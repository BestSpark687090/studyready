Craft

Literary devices glossary

DeviceDefinition
ForeshadowingHints about events that will happen later in the story
SymbolismAn object, person, or color that represents a larger idea (e.g., a storm symbolizing conflict)
IronyA contrast between expectation and reality - situational, dramatic, or verbal
MotifA recurring element (image, phrase, idea) that reinforces the work's central themes
CharacterizationHow an author reveals a character's personality - directly (stated) or indirectly (actions, dialogue, thoughts of others)
Point of viewThe narrative perspective - first person, third person limited, or third person omniscient
ToneThe author's attitude toward the subject, conveyed through word choice and style
AllegoryA narrative in which characters and events represent a broader moral, political, or social meaning

Analysis

How to identify and argue a theme

Theme is not the same as topic
"Love" is a topic. "Love requires sacrifice" is a theme - a statement the text seems to be making about that topic.
Track a pattern, not a single scene
A strong theme argument draws on at least three moments across the text, not one quote taken in isolation.
Ask what changed
Look at how a character or situation is different at the end than the beginning - that shift usually points straight at the theme.
Use the "character map" trick
Use the Concept Map Builder to connect a character to the key moments, other characters, and symbols tied to them - patterns become visible fast.

Verse

Poetry terms

TermMeaning
MeterThe rhythmic pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables (e.g., iambic pentameter)
Rhyme schemeThe pattern of end rhymes in a poem, labeled with letters (ABAB, AABB)
StanzaA grouped set of lines in a poem, similar to a paragraph in prose
EnjambmentA sentence or phrase that runs past the end of a line without a pause
ImageryDescriptive language that appeals to the five senses

Writing

Structuring a literary analysis essay

Thesis
A specific, arguable claim about the text - not a summary of the plot.
Body paragraphs
Each paragraph should argue one point, supported by a direct quote and an explanation of how that quote proves your point.
Conclusion
Restate the thesis in new words and explain why the theme matters beyond the text itself.
