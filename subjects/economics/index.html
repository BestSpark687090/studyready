Fundamentals

Supply and demand basics

Law of demand
As price rises, quantity demanded falls (all else equal) - the demand curve slopes downward.
Law of supply
As price rises, quantity supplied rises (all else equal) - the supply curve slopes upward.
Shift vs. movement
A price change causes movement along a curve. A change in another factor (income, technology, expectations) shifts the whole curve.
Surplus and shortage
A surplus happens when price is set above equilibrium (supply > demand); a shortage happens when price is set below equilibrium (demand > supply).

Common Mistakes

Where students go wrong in economics

Patterns we see again and again in student work. Each one is fixable once you know to look for it.

Confusing a shift with a movement along the curve
A change in the good's own price moves you along the demand curve. Anything else (income, tastes, price of substitutes, expectations) shifts the whole curve. This distinction is tested constantly and is the source of most wrong answers on supply and demand questions. Before answering, identify which one has changed.
Forgetting "all else equal"
Nearly every economic law assumes other factors are held constant. When a question describes two things changing at once, the laws no longer give a single answer, and the correct response is often "it depends on which effect is larger." Recognizing that is worth marks.
Mixing up nominal and real
Nominal values are measured in current money; real values are adjusted for inflation. A 5% raise during 6% inflation is a real pay cut. Any question involving change over time should prompt the question: is this nominal or real?
Treating micro and macro as the same reasoning
What is true for one household is not always true for the economy. Saving more is good for an individual; if everyone saves more at once, spending falls and so can income (the paradox of thrift). Watch for questions that ask you to scale up an individual decision.
Not drawing the graph
Supply and demand, cost curves, aggregate demand and supply: nearly every economics question is easier with a sketch. Draw it, label the axes, mark the shift, and read the answer off. Students who try to reason verbally about curves they have not drawn make far more errors.
