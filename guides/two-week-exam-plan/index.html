A Two-Week Exam Study Plan (Day by Day)

Fourteen days is enough for almost any exam if you spend them in the right order. Here is the order.

5 minute read  ·  Part of the StudyReady Guides

Two weeks out from an exam is the point where most students feel they have plenty of time, and then suddenly it is three days out and they are looking at a stack of chapters they have not touched. The plan below is designed to prevent that. It is deliberately front-loaded: the hard, unglamorous work of figuring out what you do not know happens early, so the final days are spent on targeted practice rather than panicked reading.

Adjust the numbers to your situation. A midterm covering four chapters and a final covering twelve are not the same size, but the shape of the plan is the same.

Before day 1: get the map

You cannot plan a route without a map. Spend thirty minutes making a single list of every topic the exam covers. Use the syllabus, the chapter headings, and anything your teacher has said about the format. Then, next to each topic, write one of three letters honestly:

  • K (know it): you could answer a test question on this right now.
  • S (shaky): you recognize it and could probably work it out with notes.
  • N (no): you would have to learn this from scratch.

Enter the topics into the Study Planner with the N topics due first. The most common exam-prep mistake is spending the first week on K topics because they feel good, then running out of time for the N topics that decide your grade.

Days 1 to 5: learn the N topics

Take the N topics in order and, for each, do a proper first pass: read the section, take notes (the Cornell method works well here), then immediately write questions or flashcards from those notes. Do not move to the next topic until you have written the questions. This is the step that turns reading into something reviewable.

End each day with a short closed-book recall of everything covered that day, and start each day by answering the previous day's questions. That is the spacing schedule beginning, and by day 5 you will already have reviewed day 1's material three or four times.

Aim for two topics a day at most. If you have more N topics than that allows, the exam will be hard regardless and the goal becomes triage: pick the topics most likely to carry the most marks and do those first.

Days 6 to 8: shore up the S topics

The S topics need less time each but should not be skipped. For each one, skip the full reading and go straight to a recall attempt: close the book and write everything you remember. Compare against the notes. Whatever you missed becomes a flashcard. This takes about half the time of a full first pass and is usually enough.

Keep the daily review going. By now the deck is getting large, so a fifteen to twenty minute review session each morning is normal.

Day 9: first practice test

This is the most important day of the plan and the one students most often skip. Sit a full practice exam under test conditions: timed, no notes, no phone. Use an old exam if your teacher provides one; otherwise build one from your own questions with the Practice Quiz Generator, or from the end-of-chapter questions in the textbook.

The score does not matter. What matters is the list of what you got wrong. Sort it: was each mistake because you did not know the material (a knowledge gap), because you knew it but made an error (a process problem), or because you ran out of time (a pacing problem)? Each of these gets a different fix.

Days 10 to 12: targeted repair

Go back through the practice test mistakes one at a time. Knowledge gaps go back into the day-1 rotation: reread that specific section, write new cards, review them daily. Process errors mean more practice of that specific problem type until the error stops happening. Pacing problems mean doing timed sets of the slow question types.

Keep the daily review going but let the K topics drop out of it. They are stable and you have more useful things to do with the time.

These three days are also when you should make a one-page summary of the whole exam: every formula, every key date, every definition you keep tripping on, in your own handwriting. Not to bring into the exam, but because making it is a powerful review in itself.

Day 13: second practice test

Same conditions as day 9. This time the score is informative: it tells you roughly where you will land. More importantly, compare the mistakes to day 9's mistakes. Anything you got wrong both times is the highest priority for tomorrow and should be on your one-page summary in big letters.

Day 14: the day before

Do not learn anything new. Review the one-page summary, run through your flashcard deck once, and re-solve the two or three problem types that gave you the most trouble on the practice tests. Stop by early evening.

Then sleep. Memory consolidation, the process that moves what you studied into long-term storage, happens during sleep, and it does not happen if you are up until two in the morning re-reading. A full night's sleep is worth more than any amount of last-minute review. Most students know this and stay up anyway; the ones who do not are the ones who followed a plan like this and have nothing left to cram.

Exam day

Read the whole paper before answering anything. Answer what you know first, bank those marks, and come back to the hard questions with whatever time remains. When you get the paper back, go through every mistake: the errors on a real exam are the most targeted study material you will ever get for the next one.

If you only have one week

Compress, do not skip. Days 1 to 3 on N topics, day 4 on S topics, day 5 practice test, day 6 repair, day 7 light review and sleep. The practice test is the part to protect at all costs; a week of reading with no test is far less effective than four days of reading and one honest test.
